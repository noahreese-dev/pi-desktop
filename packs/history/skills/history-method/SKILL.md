# History method

Workspace: the folder this harness starts in (read its `AGENTS.md`). At the start of a session read `INDEX.md` to see
what has already been looked at, and check `notes/` and `FINDINGS.md` before saying something is new.

## The questions to answer, in this order

1. **What is the earliest source?** Name the work, its author (or "anonymous"), when it was composed, and the exact
   place: book, chapter, section, page, line, folio or inscription number, plus a link to an edition or translation
   when one exists. Do not call a source "late" or "early" without the dates (see below).
2. **Who reports it, and to whom?** Speaker → listener at each step. Is the author an eyewitness, a contemporary
   with access, or someone writing generations later from earlier writers? Keep the voice of the original: if the
   text says "it is said" or "he was asked", do not turn it into "he said" or "he asked".
3. **Is it self-testimony?** Does a person vouch for themselves (a ruler's own inscription, a memoir), does an
   interested party vouch for them, or do outsiders report it? Say which.
4. **How many independent routes?** Mentions ≠ distinct reports ≠ independent routes. Ten chronicles copying one
   report are one report. Group by shared author, shared lost source or shared book before counting, and give the
   three numbers separately (mentions / distinct reports / independent routes), with the reason for each grouping.
   Example: Newton's falling apple (set in 1666) reaches us through William Stukeley, who wrote that Newton told him
   in 1726 (memoir written 1752, published 1936), and through Voltaire, who said he had it from Newton's niece. Two
   distinct reports, but both lead back to Newton's own household sixty years later: one route, and none from 1666.
5. **What does it establish?** An event, a belief, a text that circulated, an inference, or a religious or
   metaphysical affirmation (the four classes below).
6. **What is the other side?** Rival accounts, counterevidence, a silence that matters (a source that should
   mention it and does not), and the strongest alternative explanation.

## Four claim classes (never blur them)

- `documented_historical_event`: what happened (a death, a battle, a letter, a payment, an appointment).
- `early_community_belief`: that a named group believed something at a given time. The belief is not the event.
- `historical_inference`: a reconstruction. List the premises, the alternatives, and what would break it.
- `theological_affirmation`: miracles as miracles, divine appointment, prophecy fulfilled. History can say who
  affirmed it and when, not whether it is metaphysically true.

Do not turn "a community believed X" into "X happened", or "history cannot establish X" into "X is false".
Example: Sima Qian's *Shiji* (chapter 6, completed around 90 BCE) reports the Qin book burning of 213 BCE and the killing
of scholars the year after. That the court ordered books destroyed is a `documented_historical_event` claim with
real support; the later image of a wholesale war on Confucian scholars is partly an `early_community_belief` of Han
writers, and modern scholars debate how much of the scholars story is embellishment, a `historical_inference`
question.

## Dates: keep them separate

Event · first attested · work composed · earlier layer argued · oldest surviving manuscript · edition cited.

- Tacitus describes the fire of Rome (event 64 CE) in *Annals* 15.38-44, composed around 115-120 CE; books 11-16
  survive in essentially one medieval manuscript copied in the 11th century; you will cite a modern edition.
  Four different dates, four different questions.
- A late manuscript does not make a work late, and a late work does not make its contents early or late. Argue each
  step.

## Presenting a source

- **Raw first.** The original-language text (when available and short enough), then a faithful translation (say
  whose), then a light provenance line: the main links in the chain from event to text to manuscript to edition,
  not every name. Simpler is better. Link the source page.
- **Then the grading,** labelled exactly **HCM** (historical-critical method):
  - **Pros:** what supports it (closeness in time and place, access to the events, independent routes,
    consistency, details against the author's interest).
  - **Cons:** what weakens it (late host work, interested reporter, anonymous informant, single route,
    contradictions, anachronism, literary patterning).
  - **Assessment:** your actual judgement, with a confidence label (`unknown`, `very_low`, `low`, `moderate`,
    `high`, `very_high`) and one line on what would change it.
- **Earliest first.** Lead with the earliest and best-placed sources; put later works in their own clearly separated
  section, lower down and weighted lower.
- **Stay on the question.** If the question is one person's reliability, leave out the general background.

Example of a first-attested problem: the story of King Alfred burning the cakes is not in Asser's *Life of King
Alfred* (893), written in Alfred's lifetime. It first appears in a Life of St Neot written roughly a century after
Alfred's death (899) and entered printed editions of Asser only in 1574, when the editor added it. Asser's only
manuscript was destroyed in the Cotton library fire of 1731, so editions are reconstructed from earlier transcripts.

## Explaining

Plain words, no jargon or internal IDs. Lead with the direct answer, then why, then why not. When the user asks for it
simply, give the idea in everyday words with a tiny example, then the evidence.

## Honesty rules

- Never invent a quotation, a source, a page, a manuscript, a chain of transmission or a scholar's view. If you
  recall something but have not checked it, label it `unverified` and say where to check.
- Modern scholarship is analysis, not primary evidence. Cite it separately (author, work, year, page).
- `unknown` is a valid final answer. So is "no early independent source says this".
- Be charitable to traditions: do not reject a report by reflex or because it is pious, patriotic or convenient.
  But the historical-critical method has the final word.
- Name groups, places and people precisely and neutrally, in the terms a careful scholar from outside the
  tradition would accept, and note the tradition's own terms where they differ.
- For hard weighing (a whole family of reports, a dependence map, a final assessment), suggest the research-lanes
  skill or a stronger model.

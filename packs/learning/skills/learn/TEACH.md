# Teach: one step at a time

Working memory is small: a learner can juggle only a few new pieces at once. Every lesson is designed around that
limit. One new idea, introduced cleanly, used straight away, checked before moving on.

## Choose the step

- Look at the edge from the learning records and the mission. The next step is the smallest idea that moves them
  toward the mission and that they can reach from what they already hold.
- If the step depends on something they lack, teach that first. Write the order down for yourself (A before B before
  C) and keep to it.
- One new idea per lesson. If you find yourself writing "and also", that is the next lesson.

## Inside a lesson

1. **Connect.** One or two lines linking to something they already know (from the records or the profile). New ideas
   stick to old ones.
2. **The idea, plainly.** The shortest accurate statement, in everyday words, then the precise term. Define every
   new term the first time it appears; reuse `GLOSSARY.md` terms exactly.
3. **A worked example.** Show the idea used on a concrete case, step by step, with the reasoning written out. For
   beginners, studying a full worked example teaches more than solving a problem cold.
4. **Fade the help.** A second example with a step left for them, then a problem they do alone. Remove support as
   they succeed, not before.
5. **Check.** A question that needs the idea, not just its wording: apply it, predict, explain why, spot the error.
   Give immediate feedback that explains the reason, for right answers too.
6. **Wrap up.** One-sentence summary, one source to read or watch next, and what the next step will be.

## Keep the load down

- Put words next to the picture or code they describe; don't make the eye jump between separated parts.
- Cut what doesn't serve the idea: decorative images, tangents, history the mission doesn't need.
- Don't present the same thing twice in two forms at once (reading text aloud next to the same text).
- Use a diagram when the idea has structure (parts, flow, comparison), text when it has a sequence.
- For learners who already know the area well, skip the scaffolding: worked examples that help a beginner slow down
  an expert. Give them problems sooner.

## While teaching in chat

- One question at a time. Let them answer before explaining.
- When they're wrong, find out what they were thinking before correcting: the reason for the mistake is what to fix.
- Be honest and specific: "not quite: you added the rates; they need to be averaged by time" beats "close!".
- Praise effort and strategy that worked, not cleverness.
- When an answer shows real understanding, or fixes a misconception, write a learning record.

## Mastery before moving on

Move to the next step when they can use the idea correctly without help, not when it has been covered. If the check
fails, try a different explanation or example; don't just repeat the first one louder.
